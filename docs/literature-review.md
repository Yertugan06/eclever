# Literature review: adaptive programming practice and AI feedback for Grades 6–8

**Project:** EClever — Development of an AI-Based Adaptive and Personalized Learning Platform for Teaching Programming to Students in Grades 6–8<br>
**Working draft:** 22 October 2026<br>
**Provenance:** This review incorporates the existing [original review draft](research/original-literature-review.docx) credited there to Tsoy Ruslan and Yertugan Toktal. It adds relevant research findings and checks selected claims against the linked primary publications. It is a critical narrative review, not a systematic review or a claim about results from EClever.

## Question, selection, and evidence standard

The question is which published approaches can responsibly inform Python practice, adaptive sequencing, AI hints, and teacher support for children about 11–14 years old. The original draft selected 12 publications (S1–S12), largely from 2025–2026. This revision retains them and adds eight publications (S13–S20) on school programming pedagogy, mastery criteria, hint quality, learning dependence, motivation, and evaluation. All **20 are scientific publications** (journal articles or conference papers), although short papers and conference proceedings have weaker evidential weight than controlled studies. UNICEF guidance (G1) and the CSEDM dataset record (D1) are supplementary, **not counted** in the scientific total.

The selection is purposive: relevance to the proposed product and age group, direct programming or close transfer, and complementary positive and adverse findings. There was no reproducible database search or risk-of-bias protocol. A DOI link establishes a publication's identity; it does not independently establish every result described in the earlier draft. **Verified** below means the primary publication or author-hosted text was checked for that claim in this revision or in the linked project research. **Draft-only** means the claim is retained from the original document but its full text was not rechecked; it must not carry a design or effectiveness claim alone. Results from university students, mathematics, other languages, and other countries are explicitly treated as transfer evidence.

## Critical synthesis

### A teaching sequence is more specific than a chatbot

The strongest age-matched programming study here is PRIMM (S13). Its read-before-write progression gives EClever a defensible sequence: a worked example, prediction of output, investigation or correction, modification, then an independent writing task. S3 also indicates that teacher contact matters to children in an online course. S11 and S12 reinforce that the surrounding instructional structure matters, though their evidence is largely from university settings or heterogeneous studies. These findings justify designing practice around teacher-selected skills and graded tasks. They do **not** show that EClever's planned implementation will raise achievement; the platform has not been evaluated with students.

### Adaptation needs a cautious mastery decision

S7 suggests that a model-driven recommendation and AI explanation can play different roles. S14 makes the stronger methodological point: mastery labels depend substantially on which observations count and where the threshold is placed. For EClever, correctness from a code submission is only one observation. Hint use, repeated submissions, duplicate code, and transfer to a new task affect what that observation means. A transparent recent-performance rule is a necessary baseline for Bayesian knowledge tracing (BKT); BKT must earn its place through prospective prediction and calibration. The fixed values and threshold proposed in the requirements are **engineering hypotheses**, not established values for Kazakh school pupils. S8's project clusters show a further distinction: describing code complexity does not demonstrate that a learner has mastered the concept.

### Hints can improve a practice session while weakening independent work

The positive school reports S2 and S9 are short and not randomized. S15's AI code generation is closer to the target age but tests a different form of assistance. The randomized high-school mathematics experiment S16 provides a sharper counterexample: assisted practice can rise while unassisted performance falls. S17 and S18 add programming-specific mechanisms: learners can mistake generated help for understanding, and generic questions may be unhelpful when the actual obstacle is syntax. These findings support a progression from a specific question toward a small analogous example, with teacher access after the available hints. They do **not** establish that EClever's exact hint levels, click-through gate, or omission of target solutions will work. Those rules need leak, usability, and independent-performance tests.

### Motivation and feedback require restraint

The original draft (S3, S10, S12) treats visible progress and game mechanics as potentially motivating. That is incomplete without the competing evidence on rewards. The [meta-analysis by Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627) found that some expected tangible rewards can reduce free-choice intrinsic motivation, especially for children, while informational verbal feedback can help. This paper is an additional scientific source beyond the 20 analyzed above. EClever should show progress tied to demonstrated skills and avoid treating activity counts, streaks, or rank as mastery. S20 is directly age-relevant: a conspicuous “struggling” label or a high-friction hint request may discourage help seeking. That concern is a **design inference**, not an experimental finding about EClever's interface.

### Technical and educational outcomes are different

S2 measures engagement, S7 counts submissions, S8 describes artifacts, and S9 reports school test performance. These are not interchangeable. The [CSEDM 2019 Python challenge record](https://pslcdatashop.web.cmu.edu/DatasetInfo?datasetId=2865) (D1) provides an external sequence dataset for checking prediction methods; its 89 university students and absent ready-made skill labels limit transfer to EClever. A leakage-safe comparison should split by student and predict an attempt from earlier attempts only. Even excellent AUC or calibration on CSEDM would show predictive validity on that dataset, **not** improved learning in Kazakhstan. Hint evaluation needs separate measures of solution disclosure, factual correctness, usefulness, and human agreement; a later unassisted task would test dependency if a school pilot is available.

## Resulting project decisions and what must be tested

| Proposed EClever choice | Evidence-based rationale | Validation needed |
|---|---|---|
| Teacher-authored Python skills and a Predict → Fix/Modify → Write sequence | S13 is age-matched; S3 and S12 favor teacher-supported structured practice. | Teacher review of tasks; usability and completion data in Grades 6–8. |
| Rule-based recent-performance baseline and optional BKT for recommendations | S14 warns that evidence and threshold choices dominate model choice; S7 makes a hybrid approach plausible. | Student-held-out AUC, Brier score, calibration and false-mastery analysis; no claim of learner gain from offline metrics. |
| LLM gives levelled hints, not grading or mastery decisions | S16 shows risk from answer-giving; S17–S18 show that hint form and learner state matter. | Human-rated correctness/usefulness, automated leak tests, adversarial prompts, independent transfer items. |
| Private, neutral help and progress based on demonstrated skill | S20 directly addresses early-adolescent help avoidance; Deci et al. challenge simple reward assumptions. | Ask pupils and teachers whether labels discourage help; measure hint uptake and burden. |
| Human-readable AI disclosure before the first hint | [UNICEF Guidance on AI and Children 3.0](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/reports/policy-guidance-ai-children) (G1) recommends clear notice and caution about errors/over-reliance. | Comprehension check with the target age group; guidance is normative, not proof that a label is understood. |

## Gaps and limitations

Only a few directly relevant studies concern Python and ages 11–14 (notably S2, S9, S13, and part of S15); their settings are outside Kazakhstan. No source here establishes the quality of Russian or Kazakh hints, a safe universal session length, EClever's seven-/21-day review intervals, or a numerical mastery threshold for this population. Teacher workload effects and child understanding of AI disclosure are also unresolved. The studies use different outcomes and many short interventions; their results cannot be combined into a single expected effect size. Claims marked **draft-only** need a full-text check before quotation in the final thesis. The planned evaluation is technical unless a real school pilot is separately approved and run; consequently the project should report predictive, functional, and hint-quality results without claiming a demonstrated improvement in student learning.
